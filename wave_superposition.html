Probability Amplitude

Ψ(x,t) ∈ ℂ

The wavefunction Ψ is a complex-valued probability amplitude. It encodes all quantum information about the state. Direct measurement of Ψ is impossible — only |Ψ|² is observable.

Probability Density

ρ(x) = |Ψ(x)|² = Ψ*·Ψ

The Born rule: ρ(x) gives the probability per unit length of finding the particle at x. It is always real and non-negative.

Differential Probability

dP = |Ψ(x)|² dx

The probability of finding the particle in the infinitesimal interval [x, x+dx]. Integrating over all space gives the total probability = 1.

Normalization

∫₋∞^∞ |Ψ|² dx = 1

A valid wavefunction must be normalizable: the total probability of finding the particle somewhere must equal exactly 1. Non-normalizable states are unphysical.

Square-Integrability

Ψ ∈ L²(ℝ) ⟺ ‖Ψ‖ < ∞

Physical wavefunctions must belong to the Hilbert space L²(ℝ): the integral of |Ψ|² must converge. This excludes plane waves, which require normalization via delta functions.

Scalability / Global Phase

c·Ψ ≡ Ψ (|c|=1)

Multiplying Ψ by a complex constant c changes the wavefunction but not the physical state when |c|=1. For |c|≠1, the probability density scales by |c|², requiring renormalization.

Orthogonality

⟨m|n⟩ = ∫Ψm*·Ψn dx = 0

Two distinct eigenstates of a Hermitian operator are orthogonal — their inner product vanishes. This is the quantum analogue of perpendicular vectors.

Orthonormality

⟨m|n⟩ = δmn

A set is orthonormal if states are both orthogonal (⟨m|n⟩=0 for m≠n) and normalized (⟨n|n⟩=1). Orthonormal bases make quantum calculations tractable.

Heisenberg Uncertainty

Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2

Localizing a particle (small Δx) requires many k-components (large Δk ~ Δp/ħ). The product Δx·Δk ≥ ½ is a mathematical consequence of Fourier theory — not measurement disturbance.
